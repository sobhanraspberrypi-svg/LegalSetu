## CASE STUDY 1: GST Notice Under Section 73 (Educational Hypothetical)

**Background**:
"ABC Manufacturing Pvt Ltd" is a small enterprise in Tamil Nadu with annual turnover of ₹3.5 crore. The proprietor received a notice in Form GST DRC-01 alleging:
- Excess ITC claimed in FY 2022-23 to the extent of ₹2,40,000
- Issued under Section 73 of CGST Act
- Demanded tax, interest, and penalty

**Issue**: Was the discrepancy actually wrong, or a clerical error?

**Educational Analysis**:
1. The notice cited specific GSTR-2B vs GSTR-3B mismatch.
2. Common cause: supplier filed GSTR-1 late; ITC reflected in next quarter.
3. Section 73 is for non-fraud cases — penalty capped at 10% of tax or ₹10,000.
4. Time to respond: 30 days from notice issuance.

**Educational Outcome Pattern**:
If the discrepancy is reconciled with documentary evidence (supplier's late filings, corresponding invoice records, vendor confirmations), Section 73 notices often result in either:
- No demand (matter dropped), OR
- Demand reduced after reconciliation, OR
- Reduced penalty under Section 73(8) if paid before issuance of order

**Key Learning**:
- ITC mismatch is the most common cause of Section 73 notices.
- Maintaining vendor follow-up records is critical.
- Always reply within the time limit; non-response leads to ex-parte orders.

**Citation**: This is an illustrative case study. For actual judgments on ITC reversals, see CBIC Circulars at https://cbic-gst.gov.in/cbe-aboutus.html

## CASE STUDY 2: MSMED Act Recovery — The Importance of Udyam Date

**Background**:
"XYZ Engineering Services" (an LLP) supplied services worth ₹8.5 lakh to a large public sector enterprise in January 2023. Payment was due in 60 days but not received even after 8 months.

**The XYZ partners considered filing under MSMED Act**:
- They had Udyam registration dated July 2023 (AFTER the invoice date)
- The invoice was raised in January 2023

**Educational Analysis**:
- **Critical issue**: MSMED Act protection applies only if the supplier was a registered MSME **at the time of supply**.
- Section 2(n) defines "Supplier" as one who has filed memorandum with the authority — this must be **before** the cause of action arises.
- Therefore, XYZ could not invoke MSMED Act for this specific invoice.

**Alternative recourse**:
1. Civil suit under Indian Contract Act for breach
2. Negotiable Instruments Act if cheque was involved
3. Commercial Court (for amounts above ₹3 lakh)
4. Arbitration if contract had arbitration clause

**Key Learning**:
- **Register on Udyam Portal BEFORE entering business** — not after disputes arise.
- Once registered, all subsequent transactions enjoy MSMED protection.
- Backdating Udyam registration is NOT possible.

**Citation**: Based on Section 2(n) and Section 18 of MSMED Act 2006. Verify at https://msme.gov.in

## CASE STUDY 3: Contract Red Flag — Unilateral Termination

**Background**:
A small graphic design firm received a 2-year contract from a large brand with the following clause:

> "Section 14: Either party may terminate this Agreement for any reason or no reason by giving 30 days written notice. The Brand shall have no obligation to compensate for any work in progress at the time of termination."

**Educational Analysis**:

This clause has TWO red flags:
1. **Unilateral structure**: Although it says "either party," the practical impact is unequal. The brand has many designers; the designer firm depends heavily on this contract.
2. **No compensation for work in progress**: Designer firm could have committed resources, declined other clients, then face arbitrary termination.

**Negotiation Strategy** (Educational):
- Push for: "Termination by Brand requires 60 days notice. Designer firm shall be entitled to payment for all work completed and committed at the time of termination, prorated based on project milestones."
- Add: A "minimum guaranteed engagement" period (e.g., first 6 months non-terminable except for cause)

**Indian Law Reference**:
- Section 10 (Contract Act) requires "free consent" — gross power imbalance can affect this.
- Section 73 (Contract Act) provides compensation for losses naturally arising from breach.
- Section 74 limits penalties to reasonable compensation.

**Key Learning**:
- "Either party may terminate" clauses are often deceptive — examine the practical impact.
- Always negotiate compensation for resources committed.
- Read termination clauses BEFORE signing, not after.

## CASE STUDY 4: ITC Mismatch — A Common GST Trap

**Background**:
A small trader claimed ITC of ₹4.5 lakh in FY 2023-24 based on tax invoices from suppliers. However, GSTR-2B (auto-populated based on suppliers' GSTR-1) showed only ₹3.8 lakh. The department issued ASMT-10 notice for the ₹70,000 mismatch.

**Educational Analysis**:

**Step 1: Identify the cause**:
- Did the supplier upload invoices in GSTR-1? If not, ITC cannot be claimed (Section 16(2)(aa)).
- Did the trader actually receive the goods/services? (Section 16(2)(b))
- Was tax paid by the supplier? (Section 16(2)(c))
- Did the trader file GSTR-3B? (Section 16(2)(d))

**Step 2: Common scenarios**:
- **Supplier didn't file GSTR-1**: ITC must be reversed; pursue supplier separately.
- **Supplier filed late**: ITC available in the period when reflected in GSTR-2B.
- **Genuine ITC but missed in GSTR-3B**: Cannot claim later if time limit has passed (Section 16(4)).

**Step 3: Response strategy**:
- Reply in ASMT-11 within 30 days
- Attach: Supplier-wise reconciliation, communication with suppliers, evidence of actual receipt of goods
- If reconciliation shows mismatch is genuine error, agree and pay with interest under Section 50

**Key Learning**:
- Always reconcile GSTR-2B vs your ITC claims **monthly**, not annually.
- Section 16(4) time limit: ITC must be claimed by November of next financial year.
- Supplier follow-up is YOUR responsibility — vet suppliers before transacting.

## CASE STUDY 5: Lease Agreement Without Registration

**Background**:
A small business owner entered into a 36-month lease for commercial premises in Bengaluru. The lease was on plain paper, not registered. Three years later, when dispute arose with landlord over deposit refund, the tenant could not produce the lease as evidence in court.

**Educational Analysis**:
- Under Registration Act 1908, leases exceeding 11 months must be registered.
- Unregistered leases of longer duration are **not admissible as evidence** for terms exceeding 11 months.
- This left the tenant with limited legal protection.

**Key Learning**:
- Always register leases above 11 months
- Pay correct stamp duty (varies by state, typically 0.5-1% of total rent)
- Get the lease attested by 2 witnesses
- Keep original copy in safe storage
- For shorter periods, use 11-month leases with renewal clauses

## CASE STUDY 6: Section 132 GST — Criminal Provisions

**Background** (Educational Hypothetical):
A trader was alleged to have fraudulently availed ITC of ₹6 crore through fake invoices from non-existent firms. The department invoked Section 132 of CGST Act along with arrest provisions under Section 69.

**Educational Analysis**:
- Section 132 prescribes IMPRISONMENT for serious GST offences
- Above ₹5 crore: Up to 5 years imprisonment + fine
- This is a **cognizable** offence — police can arrest without warrant
- Bail is generally available but at the discretion of the court

**Procedural Aspects**:
1. Investigation by GST Intelligence
2. Summons under Section 70
3. Arrest under Section 69 if applicable
4. Filing of complaint in court
5. Trial under Section 132

**Key Learning**:
- GST is no longer just a "tax compliance" issue — it has criminal dimensions.
- Fake invoicing, circular trading, and fraudulent ITC carry SERIOUS consequences.
- If a Section 132 notice arrives, engage a tax lawyer (not just CA) immediately.
- Cooperate with investigation but exercise right to legal counsel.

## CASE STUDY 7: Cheque Bounce — Section 138 NI Act

**Background**:
A trader received a cheque of ₹3.5 lakh from a buyer, which was dishonoured with reason "Funds insufficient." 

**Educational Analysis** under Section 138 of Negotiable Instruments Act:

**Statutory requirements**:
1. Cheque must be towards discharge of legally enforceable debt
2. Presented within 3 months of date on cheque
3. Demand notice within 30 days of dishonour
4. Drawer fails to pay within 15 days of receiving notice
5. Complaint must be filed within 1 month after expiry of 15-day notice period

**Punishment**:
- Imprisonment up to 2 years, OR
- Fine up to twice the cheque amount, OR
- Both

**Practical Strategy**:
- Always send demand notice via Registered Post AD
- Keep proof of every step
- The case can be filed in the place where the cheque was presented for clearing
- Compoundable offence — settlement is possible at any stage

**Key Learning**:
- Don't accept post-dated cheques without verifying drawer's financial standing
- Cheque issued for past dues should have an underlying acknowledgement (writing)
- "Stop payment" instruction is also a Section 138 offence (Supreme Court precedent)
